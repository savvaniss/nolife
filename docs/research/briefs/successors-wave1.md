# First-wave Second Life rivals and successors (2000s-2010s): why they failed

Research brief for the "nolife" design programme. Compiled 2026-10-09.

**Method note.** 16 web searches (extended mode) were run; the extracts below quote the underlying pages as surfaced by search. Direct WebFetch of primary pages failed in this sandbox (DNS resolution blocked for every host tried: Wikipedia, VentureBeat, Gamasutra/GameDeveloper, Engadget, TechCrunch, UploadVR, ryanschultz.com, modemworld.me) and the shared search budget was exhausted before follow-up queries on There.com funding, Kaneva funding, PS Home revenue and Cloud Party funding could run. Facts are therefore cited to the page the search surfaced; figures tagged "unverified" come from aggregators, fan wikis or AI-generated encyclopedias and should be re-checked before being quoted externally. Company self-reports are labelled as such.

## Findings

### 1. Consumer 3D worlds that ran out of money (There.com, Blue Mars, Kaneva, Twinity, Frenzoo)

**There.com (2003-2010; relaunched 2012).** Lagging growth led to major layoffs in August 2004 and a split into two entities: Forterra Systems (government simulation) and Makena Technologies (the consumer product); some sources date the spin-off to 2005 (Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There_(virtual_world)). CEO Michael Wilson announced the closure about a week ahead of the 9 March 2010 shutdown, pointing to shrinking revenue even though membership kept growing, and blaming the recession; a critic noted that the loss of a few key sponsors plus declining developer and real-estate fees tipped the company over (Source: https://venturebeat.com/2010/03/03/virtual-world-there-com-shutting-down-march-9/; https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/download/656/656/653). Users had migrated "in droves" to Facebook-era social networks (Source: https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/download/656/656/653). Refunds were limited to Therebucks bought in a set window and ended 1 April 2010 (Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There_(virtual_world)). The world relaunched on 2 May 2012 under a US$10/month premium subscription with free/low-cost trial tiers, after an earlier May 2011 plan for an 18+ cloud-based reopening (Source: https://there.blog/there-com-frequently-asked-questions/; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There_(virtual_world)). Displaced residents regrouped on forums, Facebook groups, YouTube and Second Life (Source: https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/download/656/656/653). A YouTube documentary in April 2025 (14M+ views) triggered a surge of returning players, evidence that the community outlived the business model (Source: https://there.blog/there-com-frequently-asked-questions/).

**Blue Mars (Avatar Reality, 2009-2012).** Founded December 2006 in Honolulu by Henk Rogers and Kazuyuki Hashimoto (ex-Squaresoft CTO, ex-EA VP); built on Crytek's CryEngine 2 for "much more realistic graphics than Second Life" (Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avatar_Reality; https://ryanschultz.com/2018/01/02/editorial-lessons-for-sansar-from-the-failure-of-blue-mars/). Open beta launched October 2009; land sales to third-party developers began January 2010; a $4.2M round in March 2010 brought total invested to "over $13 million" (Source: https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/avatar-reality-secures-4-2m-appoints-design-vp). By January 2011 staffing had been cut by two-thirds to about 10 people; CEO Jim Sink announced the end of PC-client development and a shift to iOS, leaving the company himself (Source: http://www.metaversejournal.com/2011/01/15/the-partial-death-and-refocus-of-blue-mars/). The iOS app and a "Lite" PC/Mac chatroom with Google Street View backdrops both failed; Hashimoto left in March 2012 and production effectively ceased in 2012, with technology rights (valued at $10M in R&D) granted to Ball State University's IDIA Lab (Source: https://www.gamebusiness.jp/article/2012/03/06/5588.html; https://ryanschultz.com/2018/01/02/editorial-lessons-for-sansar-from-the-failure-of-blue-mars/). Critics' causes: engineering effort spent on non-core features (bots before basic avatar customisation), a tiny pool of default animations, "zero press", and a target audience that drifted from big business to new users to teenage gamers (Source: https://ryanschultz.com/2018/01/02/editorial-lessons-for-sansar-from-the-failure-of-blue-mars/). No system-requirement figures were retrievable; the CryEngine 2 base implies gaming-class PCs in 2009, which is the widely cited but here unverified "graphics too heavy" complaint.

**Kaneva (2004-2016).** The world and its social-community site closed 14 November 2016; the reason is undocumented and the company became Kava, LLC, publishing mobile/Facebook games such as CasinoLife Poker (Source: https://virtual-worlds.fandom.com/wiki/Kaneva; https://www.virtualworlds.museum/exhibits/kaneva). An aggregator claims a 60% layoff and refocus on casual games, citing Gamasutra, but this is unverified (Source: https://www.highperformr.ai/company/kaneva).

**Twinity (Metaversum GmbH, Berlin, 2008-).** A 2012-era academic paper described Twinity as still in beta with technical problems and "only a few users"; in January 2012 portions of the world were taken offline for unspecified reasons; in 2012, after declining usage, Metaversum was acquired by ExitReality (Source: https://www.engadget.com/2012-01-11-portions-of-twinity-virtual-world-taken-offline.html; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaversum_GmbH). The Virtual Worlds Museum lists it as running 2008-2021 (single source, unverified) (Source: https://www.virtualworlds.museum/exhibit/sunset-worlds).

**Frenzoo.** A fashion-focused world for a young female audience, closed in 2011 or 2012 so the company could become an iOS/Android app developer (Source: https://virtual-worlds.fandom.com/wiki/Category:Defunct_Virtual_Worlds).

### 2. Big-company bets shut for strategic reasons (The Sims Online, PlayStation Home, Google Lively, Cloud Party)

**The Sims Online / EA-Land (2002-2008).** Peaked at roughly 105,000 players in 2003 against far higher EA expectations, then fell sharply (Source: https://www.thesimsnews.com/feature/server-shutdowns-and-cancelled-titles-4-lost-sims-titles-canned-before-the-sims-mobile/). EA cut the box price to $29.99 within months but kept a ~$10 monthly fee; critics cite "a complete lack of customizable features" and no new content (Source: https://www.thesimsnews.com/feature/server-shutdowns-and-cancelled-titles-4-lost-sims-titles-canned-before-the-sims-mobile/; https://sims.fandom.com/wiki/The_Sims_Online). The EA-Land rebrand promised Second Life-style customisation, land ownership and a free client; it never fully shipped, and EA announced the end in April 2008 and closed servers on 1 August 2008, saying only "the lifetime of the game has drawn to an end" (Source: https://techcrunch.com/?p=16927; https://sims.fandom.com/wiki/The_Sims_Online). A claim that EA cited "unsustainable subscription revenue" appears only in an AI-generated encyclopedia and is unverified (Source: https://grokipedia.com/page/The_Sims_Online).

**PlayStation Home (2008-2015).** Wikipedia's infobox lists 41 million members at closure; a blog cited 31M in March 2013 and a petition claimed 32M+; the 41M figure traces to wiki sources rather than a Sony release and should be treated as approximate cumulative registrations, not actives (Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PlayStation_Home; https://www.jamiebalfour.scot/blog/posts/playstation-home-to-shut-down). Sony announced the shutdown in September 2014 citing a "shifting [market] landscape"; new content ceased in November 2014 and servers closed 31 March 2015 (Source: https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/today-s-the-day-sony-shuts-down-playstation-home-for-good; https://www.forbes.com/sites/insertcoin/2014/09/27/sony-finally-shutting-down-playstation-home-on-ps3/). Sony had stopped paying Home revenue to developers "some time" before; nDreams redirected its Home earnings into VR R&D (Source: https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/today-s-the-day-sony-shuts-down-playstation-home-for-good). Forbes: "outside of a niche community, it did not catch on with the larger PlayStation public" (Source: https://www.forbes.com/sites/insertcoin/2014/09/27/sony-finally-shutting-down-playstation-home-on-ps3/). Home was never ported to PS4; the fan project Destination Home resurrected it offline and later online on modded PS3s/emulators (Source: https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/6-years-after-it-was-shut-down-playstation-home-has-been-resurrected-by-fans/). No Sony revenue figures were found.

**Google Lively (July-December 2008).** Google announced the closure in November 2008 "to prioritize our resources and focus more on our core search, ads and apps business", acknowledging that "not every bet is going to pay off"; rooms held at most 20 people; the client ran on Windows only (not Mac, Linux, or Chrome); roughly 10,000 active users at cancellation (unverified, secondary) (Source: https://www.gamedeveloper.com/pc/google-lively-to-shut-down-in-december; https://www.failory.com/google/lively; https://blogs.cetis.org.uk/rowin/2008/11/20/lively-limps-out-of-the-muve-game/index.html).

**Cloud Party (2012-2014).** A plug-in-free, browser-based 3D world with user-built content, founded by MMO/console veterans; CEO Sam Thompson announced on 24 January 2014 that the team was joining Yahoo and the service would close 21 February 2014, with an export guide for users; financial terms undisclosed; press read it as an acqui-hire (Source: https://techcrunch.com/2014/01/24/yahoo-acquires-will-shut-down-browser-based-virtual-world-gaming-startup-cloud-party; https://www.pcworld.com/article/443557/yahoo-buys-virtual-worlds-gaming-company-cloud-party.html). It is listed among Marissa Mayer's 53 acquisitions, none of which produced a virtual-world product (Source: https://gizmodo.com/heres-what-happened-to-all-of-marissa-mayers-yahoo-acqu-1781980352).

### 3. The VR wave (High Fidelity, Sansar, vTime)

**High Fidelity (2013-2019).** Raised roughly $73M, including a $35M round in June 2018 (Source: https://variety.com/2019/digital/news/high-fidelity-layoffs-1203208860/). Rosedale said he had expected 10-20 million people using HMDs daily by 2019 and the real figure was "close to two orders of magnitude less"; the in-world economy needed concurrent user numbers it could not reach (Source: https://modemworld.me/2019/04/11/high-fidelity-changes-direction-the-reality-of-vr-worlds-today-tomorrow/). Spaces could technically host up to 500 people; a company blog documented 90 concurrent in one space (Source: https://www.highfidelity.com/backlog/creating-crowds-in-vr-eabbc325dcfc). May 2019: 20 staff (about 25%) laid off and focus shifted to desktop-viewable virtual offices (Source: https://venturebeat.com/2019/05/07/high-fidelity-vr-firm-lays-off-25-focuses-on-virtual-offices/). December 2019: half of remaining staff cut, apps withdrawn from Steam and Oculus stores, the open-source codebase closed, marketplace and currency shut down, effective 15 January 2020; Rosedale wrote that "the existing team was not the one to carry the new project forward"; the company became a spatial-audio vendor (Source: https://ryanschultz.com/2019/12/11/high-fidelity-suspends-its-remote-teamworks-project-announces-more-staff-layoffs-closes-its-open-source-codebase-and-withdraws-its-apps/; https://www.hypergridbusiness.com/2019/12/high-fidelity-shutting-down/; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Fidelity,_Inc.).

**Sansar (Linden Lab, 2017-2020; Wookey 2020-).** Public beta July 2017 as a VR-native successor to Second Life (Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sansar_(video_game)). Peak concurrency "remained in the low hundreds at most" despite Hello Kitty, Ready Player One and esports campaigns (Source: https://nwn.blogs.com/nwn/2019/11/sansar-linden-lab-layoff-social-vr-.html). Over 20 Sansar staff cut in November 2019 and more in February 2020 (one blog claimed 40+, unconfirmed); CEO Ebbe Altberg said Linden Lab "couldn't continue to sponsor Sansar financially" (Source: https://nwn.blogs.com/nwn/2019/11/sansar-linden-lab-layoff-social-vr-.html; https://ryanschultz.com/2020/02/11/linden-lab-lays-off-40-staff-shuts-down-the-sansar-project/). Sold to Wookey Project Corp on 23-25 March 2020 with Tilia contracted for payments; Wookey pivoted to live events, suffered funding shortfalls and a CEO lawsuit (Jonathan Fried, unpaid salary/relocation), and the whole service went offline for a period in December 2021 (Source: https://roadtovr.com/sansar-spin-off-linden-lab-refocus-second-life/; https://modemworld.me/2021/12/29/sansar-the-wookey-in-ex-ceo-lawsuit/; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sansar_(video_game)). Linden Lab itself was acquired by an investor group led by Randy Waterfield and Brad Oberwager in July 2020 (Source: https://ryanschultz.com/2020/07/09/linden-lab-to-be-acquired-by-an-investment-group-led-by-randy-waterfield-and-brad-oberwager/). The often-quoted "$75M spent on Sansar" figure was not found in any surfaced source and is unverified.

**vTime / vTime XR (Liverpool, 2015-2025).** Bootstrapped by Martin Kenwright, then a $7.6M (GBP 5.4M) Series A in April 2018 led by Deepbridge Capital with MSIF (Source: https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/vr-social-network-vtime-closes-76-million-series-a-funding-round-679378133.html). Rebranded vTime XR in 2019 to add AR (Source: https://vtime.net/press/2019/vtime-becomes-vtime-xr-the-worlds-first-cross-reality-xr-social-experience/). Closed abruptly in October 2025, citing an unpatchable Unity security vulnerability, with no farewell event allowed (Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VTime_XR; https://ryanschultz.com/tag/vtime/). The query's assumed 2023 date and Starship Group funding were not supported.

### 4. Survivors and their pivots (Active Worlds, Entropia, Habbo, IMVU)

**Active Worlds (1995-).** Launched 28 June 1995 from Ron Britvich's 1994 WebWorld/AlphaWorld; grew 1995-2001 across ~700 worlds; still running, latest release 8.2 (May 2023), but a visitor reports "the same fifteen people are always on" and tracker estimates range from ~90 to a few thousand daily (unreliable) (Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_Worlds; https://massivelyop.com/2020/06/21/the-game-archaeologist-the-virtual-worlds-of-the-1990s/; https://mmostats.com/game/active-worlds). Survival mode: tiny team, low fixed costs, loyal builders.

**Entropia Universe (MindArk, 2002-).** Commercial open trial 20 May 2002, gold January 2003; PED fixed at 10:1 USD and withdrawable; MindArk managers bought the company back from a receiver in April 2003; 500,000 registered users by October 2006; $165M "passed through" in 2005 and $360M turnover claimed for 2006; Guinness records for a $26,500 island (2004) and $100,000 Club NEVERDIE (2008), later a $330,000 space station (2009) and $635,000 Club Neverdie resale in pieces (2010); company-reported $450M transactions in 2020 and, cumulatively to 2025, $242M deposits vs $64M withdrawals; moving to Unreal Engine 5 (deal June 2021, migration still under way in 2026) (Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropia_Universe; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MindArk; https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/mindark-pe-ab---metrics-of-a-virtual-world-with-boundless-opportunities-discover-the-world-of-entropia-universe-302341829.html). All volume figures are self-reported and unaudited; the deposit/withdrawal gap shows the economy is net-extractive from players.

**Habbo (Sulake, 2000-).** Revenue up 20%+ to $39M in H1 2010 and $78.7M for 2011 (Source: https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/habbo-hotel-dev-s-revenues-jump-20-percent-after-cost-savings-; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sulake). A June 2012 Channel 4 investigation into child-safety moderation led Balderton (13%) and 3i to dump stakes and advertisers to leave; Sulake muted all chat, revenue collapsed and only recovered to ~75% by August; headcount went from ~90 to 30 by October 2012 with all 11 country offices closed; Elisa took control in February 2013; Hotel Hideaway (2018-) is the mobile follow-on (Source: https://techcrunch.com/2012/06/12/trouble-at-habbo-hotel-balderton-dumps-13-stake-in-owner-sulake-over-dodgy-child-content/; https://forum.ragezone.com/threads/interview-sulake-15-years-of-habbo-hotel.1060525/; https://habboxwiki.com/Sulake).

**IMVU (2004-; Together Labs).** Founded 2004; pivoted early from a 3D-chat plug-in to "meet new people" after user feedback; $25M revenue run-rate in October 2009, $40M by April 2010 with 75% from credits for user-made goods; "profitable with an annual revenue rate of more than $55 million" (company claim, c. 2020); 7M+ monthly actives and 400,000 user-created products per month in 2020; $35M raised January 2021 from NetEase and Structural Capital under the new Together Labs parent, plus a VCOIN digital asset (Source: https://www.dfcint.com/dossier/imvu-banks-on-chat/; https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/imvu-inc-celebrates-nine-years-of-connecting-people-around-the-world-expands-profitable-business-to-mobile-201920201.html; https://techcrunch.com/2021/01/25/imvu-35-million-funding/). Registered-account claims range 50M-111M depending on year; treat as marketing.

## Patterns / root causes

1. **Purpose vacuum.** Every pure "hang out in 3D" product (There, Lively, Home, Kaneva, Twinity, Blue Mars, Sansar, High Fidelity, vTime) died; every survivor has a concrete loop: gambling-like resource economy (Entropia), teen status goods and room decoration (Habbo), avatar-mediated dating/chat with a creator economy (IMVU), hobby building (Active Worlds). Sony's own verdict ("niche community") and Forbes's reading of Home say the same thing: a lobby is not a reason to log in.

2. **Concurrency, not registrations, is the vital sign.** Home's 41M "members" and IMVU's 111M "accounts" are cumulative sign-ups. The numbers that predicted death were Sansar's "low hundreds" concurrent, High Fidelity's 90-in-a-room record, Lively's 20-per-room cap and ~10k actives, and TSO's 105k peak subscribers. Rosedale explicitly tied economic viability to concurrency.

3. **Wrong hardware bet, twice.** Blue Mars (CryEngine 2 in 2009) and the VR trio (High Fidelity, Sansar, vTime 2016-2019) each required hardware the audience did not own. Rosedale's own estimate: daily VR users were ~100x below his plan. Each then pivoted to lighter clients (Blue Mars Lite/iOS, High Fidelity desktop, vTime XR) too late.

4. **Platform dependence kills on the owner's schedule.** Lively (Google core focus), Home (Sony's PS4 transition and "shifting landscape"), Cloud Party (Yahoo acqui-hire), Sansar (Linden Lab refocusing on Second Life), vTime (Unity vulnerability) were all closed by decisions outside the community. Habbo nearly died from a single TV investigation plus investor flight.

5. **Fixed-cost economics vs. sponsor/land revenue.** There.com died with growing membership because sponsor, developer and land fees shrank in the 2008-2010 recession; Blue Mars burned $13M+ before selling meaningful land; Sansar could not be "sponsored" by Second Life's cash flow. Worlds whose revenue is tied to users' own spending on each other (IMVU credits, Habbo furni, Entropia deposits) survived.

6. **Subscription walls and box prices stall worlds.** TSO's $49.99 box plus $10/month collapsed in a year; There's 2012 return as a $10/month club is a hobby, not a growth business. The successful models were free-to-enter with paid goods.

7. **Content tooling and creator economics decide stickiness.** TSO lacked customisation; Blue Mars shipped bots before avatar customisation and had almost no animations; Cloud Party and Second Life retained builders. IMVU's 400k products/month and Entropia's seven-figure asset sales show that letting users own and resell is the engine.

8. **Moderation is existential, not a feature.** Habbo lost two investors, most advertisers, ~25% of revenue and two-thirds of staff in one summer over child-safety failures. Any successor aimed at adults still inherits this risk at scale.

9. **Communities outlive companies.** There's residents regrouped on Facebook and returned en masse in 2025; PS Home was resurrected by fans; Active Worlds persists with a skeleton crew. Shutting down without export tools or farewell events (vTime) is remembered as betrayal; Cloud Party's export guide is the counter-example.

10. **Pivot chains signal death.** Blue Mars (business -> new users -> teens -> iOS -> Lite), High Fidelity (VR -> desktop offices -> audio), Sansar (SL successor -> events), Kaneva (world -> casino apps): each successive pivot shed users and staff.

## Design implications for nolife

- Design the purpose loop before the renderer: a day-one reason to return (collaborative building with ownership, events with scarcity, a tradable economy) measured by peak concurrent users per region, not sign-ups.
- Ship for hardware the audience already has: a web/desktop/mobile-first client with scalable fidelity, and VR as an optional view, never a requirement. High-end graphics must degrade gracefully to integrated GPUs and phones.
- Make the economy user-to-user (creator goods, land rent paid to creators, event tickets) so revenue scales with community activity rather than with sponsors or ad budgets; avoid dependence on a handful of brand partners.
- No subscription wall at entry; free-to-enter, with premium tiers for creators and land, following IMVU/Habbo/Second Life rather than TSO/There-2012.
- Build creator tooling (avatar customisation, animation library, in-world editing, import pipelines) as the core product; ship bots, AI NPCs and spectacle only once the basic self-expression loop is excellent.
- Bake in trust and safety from the first release: age-gating, verified-adult spaces, moderation tooling and transparent reporting, because one Channel 4-style exposé can remove investors and advertisers in weeks.
- Guarantee portability and continuity: user-exportable assets and inventories, open formats, an escrowed "sunset plan" (server images, export tools, community handover) published in advance, so the community is never hostage to a corporate pivot or an engine vulnerability.
- Avoid single-vendor platform lock-in (console store, one engine, one headset ecosystem); treat engine choice and store distribution as replaceable layers.
- Keep fixed costs low enough to survive a recession on organic revenue (Active Worlds model) while funding growth features from creator-economy take rates.
- Plan for concurrency at the technical level (hundreds in one space, thousands per event) since the biggest shared moments are what the survivors monetise.
