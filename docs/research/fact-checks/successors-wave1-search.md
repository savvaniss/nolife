# Search verification: successors-wave1 (first-wave Second Life rivals)

Date: 2026-10-09. Checker: independent fact-checker with live WebSearch.

## Method note

- 12 WebSearch calls (hard cap), all in standard mode; one per claim, two for Entropia (deposits/withdrawals, then Guinness dates). Budget exhausted after the 12th call.
- Page fetching (WebFetch/curl) is blocked in this sandbox. Every verdict below rests on the quoted extracts the search engine returned from the cited pages, not on a direct read of the page. Where an extract did not surface a figure, I say so and label it prior knowledge.
- The two earlier checkers had no search access; their notes were treated as hypotheses to test, not as evidence.

## Additional findings relevant to designing a successor world

1. Habbo's contraction preceded the scandal: Sulake announced a 25% workforce cut and closure of all 11 country offices in February 2012, four months before the Channel 4 broadcast. The June 2012 investor exits (Balderton 13% to zero, 3i 16%) and the October 2012 plan to cut up to 60 of 90 jobs then compounded an existing decline. "One exposé removed two-thirds of staff" overstates causality; the safer lesson is that a safety scandal hits hardest when a company is already shrinking.
2. Sansar's real engagement numbers were an order of magnitude below "low hundreds": community-tracked average concurrency in the tens, peak ~220, and public experiences rarely above 50 visitors. A successor's go/no-go threshold should be set against those numbers.
3. PS Home economics: a developer co-founder said Home was profitable but Sony "wasn't interested in smaller profit margins" and the cost of running and supporting it had grown beyond Sony's expectation. This partially answers the open question on profitability (anecdotal, not a Sony figure).
4. The Sims Online's 105k peak is contested; one account gives ~15,000 active monthly subscribers in 2003. If the lower number is right, the subscription-wall failure was even starker.
5. MindArk's own filings: an average depositing user deposits more than $8,000 over their lifetime, and depositors are ~10% of active users; withdrawals carry a 1% fee and a 1,000 PED ($100) minimum. The Jan 2025 release also claims six player-run companies listed on an in-world exchange (combined market cap $25.4M) and 1.5M "nanotransactions" per hour. All self-reported.
6. Club NEVERDIE resale: parcels sold for a total of $635,000 in November 2010 (535% return in five years), evidence that scarce, revenue-generating virtual property can appreciate when the economy is withdrawable.
7. IMVU has raised >$77M across five rounds since 2004; a 2024 profile still cites ~7M MAU, suggesting a plateau rather than growth after the 2021 round.
8. High Fidelity's miss is framed differently by outlets: Modem World quotes 10-20 million daily HMD users predicted in 2013; VentureBeat says "millions" predicted in 2014 with actual daily users "in the tens of thousands". Either way, the gap is ~100x, and the brief's point stands.
9. Blue Mars' $13M came largely from a Hawaii VC (Kolohala Ventures) and co-founder Henk Rogers, i.e. founder-adjacent money rather than broad VC syndicates, which may explain why it could not refinance after the CryEngine bet failed.
10. There.com reopened in 2012 with two free-trial tiers alongside the $10/month subscription, so it was not a pure paywall; the brief's "hobby club" framing is fair but should note the trial tiers.
11. Cloud Party's export guide (confirmed) and Sansar's survival via a sale with 30 staff re-hired are the two concrete precedents for the "escrowed sunset plan" design implication.
